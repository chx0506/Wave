# ACOG cycle care reference brief

Sources:
- ACOG FAQ046, Dysmenorrhea: Painful Periods, last reviewed January 2022.
- ACOG FAQ057, Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS), last reviewed November 2025.
- ACOG FAQ095, Abnormal Uterine Bleeding, last reviewed August 2025.
- ACOG FAQ Heavy Menstrual Bleeding, patient FAQ.

Use boundary:
- Use this brief for educational, non-diagnostic cycle guidance.
- Do not diagnose PMS, PMDD, endometriosis, fibroids, anemia, or any other condition.
- Personal advice should be framed as gentle observation and self-care, not medical certainty.
- Mention professional care when symptoms are severe, worsening, unusual for the user, or disruptive.

Cycle and bleeding notes:
- A menstrual cycle is counted from the first day of menstrual bleeding to the first day of the next menstrual bleeding.
- For many adults, cycle length is commonly described around 21-35 days.
- A period commonly lasts up to 7 days.
- Bleeding may be described by the user as spotting, light, medium, heavy, or flooding; trend and disruption matter more than a single label.
- Heavier bleeding signals include needing to change a pad or tampon every hour for several hours, bleeding longer than 7 days, changing products overnight, or clots about quarter-size or larger.
- Urgent care is appropriate when very heavy bleeding is paired with dizziness, lightheadedness, chest pain, shortness of breath, faintness, or racing pulse.

Pain and menstrual phase notes:
- Menstrual pain is common, especially for 1-2 days around bleeding.
- Cramping pain before or during a period is linked to prostaglandins, which can make the uterus contract.
- Prostaglandin levels tend to be higher at the start of bleeding and often lessen after the first few days.
- Pain can appear with nausea, diarrhea, headache, dizziness, fatigue, or sleep disruption.
- Warmth on the abdomen or lower back, enough sleep, relaxation, yoga, and regular gentle aerobic movement may help some people cope with period discomfort.
- Pain that gets worse over time, lasts longer than usual cramps, does not improve with usual care, or disrupts daily life deserves medical attention.

Premenstrual and luteal notes:
- Physical or mood changes before menstruation can include irritability, anxiety, sadness, poor concentration, insomnia, more napping, appetite changes, breast tenderness, bloating, headache, aches, fatigue, skin changes, gastrointestinal symptoms, or abdominal pain.
- PMS is a repeated pattern that occurs before periods and interferes with normal life; diagnosis needs symptom tracking over multiple cycles.
- Mood or sleep changes that are severe, relationship-disrupting, work-disrupting, or present outside the premenstrual window should not be reduced to the cycle alone.
- Helpful app guidance can invite tracking, sleep protection, lower-friction tasks, calmer food choices, hydration, and a clear plan for seeking care if symptoms are intense.
