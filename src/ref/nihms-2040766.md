## The Menstrual Cycle as a Vital Sign: a comprehensive review

**Ana K. Rosen Vollmar, PhD**a, **Shruthi Mahalingaiah, MD**b,c, **Anne Marie Jukic, PhD**a aNational Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, Epidemiology Branch, Durham, NC, 27709, USA

# Abstract

Some medical professional organizations have advocated for including the menstrual cycle as a vital sign in adolescence, but not in adulthood. However, documenting menstrual cycle patterns is not routine clinical or research practice. Vital signs are used to predict health outcomes, indicate needed treatment, and monitor a clinical course. They can help identify pathologies, affirm wellness, and are responsive to exposures. Here we review the scientific evidence showing how the menstrual cycle meets these criteria and should therefore be treated as a vital sign. Using key words and controlled vocabulary terms, we carried out multiple literature searches, 

Author Manuscriptprioritizing the inclusion of systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical practice guidelines. 

This review describes how the menstrual cycle is a health indicator, can cyclically impact health conditions, and its associations with long-term post-menopausal health outcomes. We review exposures influencing the menstrual cycle, evidence underlying its use to optimize wellness, and available tools for documenting cycles. Supplementary materials include patient handouts on menstrual cycle tracking, and an index of related clinical practice guidelines and reviews by subject. The menstrual cycle is a vital sign from menarche through menopause, an underutilized but powerful tool for understanding gynecological and general health.

# Introduction

In 2006, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) advocated using the menstrual cycle as a vital sign for girls and adolescents, allowing early identification of health conditions with abnormal menstrual patterns and early interventions to improve adult health outcomes (1, 2). ACOG reaffirmed its opinion in 2015 and 2021. Since these committee opinions were published, smartphone cycle tracking applications (apps) proliferated (3); menstrual equity laws were passed to 

### Author Manuscript address period poverty and improve menstrual product accessibility, affordability, and safety 

(4); and thousands of individuals reported COVID-19 mRNA vaccines seemed to impact their cycles, spurring research and highlighting the fact that cycle information is rarely collected in vaccine trials (5). Menstrual health is of critical public health importance, directly impacting the health and wellbeing of people who menstruate, and requiring the education and involvement of those who do not experience the menstrual cycle (6, 7). The Global Menstrual Collective defines menstrual health as “a state of physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity, in relation to the menstrual cycle” (7). Treating the menstrual cycle as a vital sign has the potential to increase access to accurate information across the lifespan and improve timely diagnosis and treatment of menstrual health-related conditions (7).Yet, documenting the menstrual cycle as a vital sign is still not routine clinical practice.

### Author Manuscript In this review, we assess the evidence for treating the menstrual cycle as a vital sign 

from menarche to menopause. We describe how the menstrual cycle is a health indicator, associated with long-term health outcomes, and used to optimize wellness. We review external exposures that can influence the menstrual cycle, and health conditions that can vary with it. We also summarize available tools for documenting cycles. While ACOG and AAP advocated using the menstrual cycle as a vital sign for children and adolescents, based on the evidence we argue it should be considered a vital sign through menopause. In this review, we use the inclusive terms “menstruator” (8) and “people with a menstrual cycle”.

## Vital signs Although vital signs are a widely accepted component of monitoring health, to our knowledge no major international medical organization has issued a definition of what constitutes a vital sign, and definitions in peer-reviewed literature vary, despite widespread practice consensus that the four classic vital signs are pulse, temperature, respiratory rate, and blood pressure (9–13). In this review, we use Kellett’s (2017) definition of a vital sign as “any patient feature that predicts outcome, indicates the need for specific treatment, or which can be used to monitor clinical course” (14). Multiple reviews on vital signs note 

### Author Manuscript there is a lack of research on vital signs, including their value to clinical monitoring, ability 

to predict short- and long-term clinical outcomes, and optimal measurement practices (9, 15–17). These reviews do come to a common consensus, however, that “much of current practice of vital sign measurement is based more on tradition and expert opinion than on research findings” (9).

Including the menstrual cycle as a vital sign underscores its fundamental importance to health across the lifespan and potential to improve diagnosis and treatment of menstrual health-related conditions. In addition to predicting health outcomes and indicating treatment and clinical needs (11, 14, 15), vital signs help identify pathologies, affirm wellness, and are responsive to external exposures. They are dynamic: the classic vital signs can change over seconds to minutes. The menstrual cycle has a longer timeframe of days to months, but is also dynamic. Although information from a single point in time can be useful, trends 

### Author Manuscript over time are key to interpreting vital signs (14, 15, 18). The same is true of the menstrual 

cycle: although information from a single cycle can be useful, it cannot capture variability or patterns over time (19). Just as the classic vital signs regularly are altered with, for example, antihypertensives and exercise, the menstrual cycle is also routinely altered with medications and hormones.

## The menstrual cycle The menstrual cycle is a series of interdependent cyclic physiologic events. In a typical cycle, the hypothalamus releases pulses of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), leading to the release of follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) from the pituitary gland (20). During the follicular phase, FSH stimulates the growth of ovarian follicles. As the follicles develop, they secrete increasing levels of estradiol which 

### Author Manuscript stimulates endometrial proliferation (21). At the same time, rising estradiol leads to a mid-

cycle LH surge, which triggers ovulation. The luteal phase begins as the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum which secretes multiple hormones, especially progesterone which stimulates decidualization of the endometrium. With corpus luteum involution, progesterone declines triggering menstruation and the start of a new menstrual cycle (20– 22).

### Author Manuscript Commonly tracked external indicators reflecting the internal hormonal changes of the 

menstrual cycle include vaginal bleeding, basal body temperature (BBT), cervical fluid or mucus, and urinary levels of LH, and estradiol, progesterone, and their metabolites (21). BBT is measured upon waking at approximately the same time each day (23). It is approximately 0.5 °F (0.3 °C) lower in the follicular than the luteal phase (21, 22). After ovulation, increasing progesterone raises BBT until 1–2 days before menses, when BBT falls. Daily BBT tracking can be used to confirm ovulation retrospectively. Changes to cervical fluid across the menstrual cycle are easily observed at the vulva and cervix (24). In ovulatory cycles, cervical fluid changes in sensation (from dry, to moist, to wet, to slippery), color (from white/yellow to clear), and consistency (from tacky, to creamy, to stretchy) (25, 26). It is produced most abundantly in the days just before ovulation, and the last day of clear, stretchy (at least 1 inch), and slippery cervical fluid occurs within about 24 hours of ovulation in 75% of menstrual cycles (25, 27). As estrogen levels fall after ovulation 

### Author Manuscript

and progesterone rises, cervical fluid disappears and menstruators experience dry days for the rest of the luteal phase (22, 27). At-home urinary hormone testing devices can also be used to monitor the menstrual cycle. A peak in estrone-3-glucuronide (E1G), a urinary metabolite of estradiol, is used to identify the beginning of the fertile window; an LH surge typically indicates peak fertility, though may not be present before ovulation; and increasing pregnanediol-3 glucuronide (PdG), a urinary metabolite of progesterone, is used to confirm ovulation and mark the start of the luteal phase (28). Although urinary hormone testing can accurately identify cycle phases, ovulation, and the fertile window (28, 29), the devices are prohibitively expensive and may not be readily available in all regions. See Supplementary Materials for educational handouts on tracking the menstrual cycle.

## Defining a normal menstrual cycle Author Manuscript

The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) defines normal menstrual cycles as occurring every 24–38 days (30). Menstrual bleeding lasts ≤8 days with a normal flow volume as assessed by the menstruator, without intermenstrual bleeding. Cycle length typically varies, but not by more than 7–9 days (30). Abnormal menstrual cycles are long (\>38 days), short (\<24 days), or absent. Bleeding lasts \>8 days and is either light or heavy, and intermenstrual bleeding may be random or cyclic. Cycles are considered irregular if they differ in length by more than 8–10 days (30). In general, the follicular phase is longer than the luteal phase. Studies estimate follicular phase length to range from 

14.7 to 18.8 days and the luteal phase from 11.7 to 13.5 days (27, 31–33). The biological fertile window, based on the lifespan of both sperm and ovum, is 6 days long, ending with the day of ovulation (34). The clinical fertile window, defined by the presence of cervical fluid, ranges from 9 to 12.5 days (26, 27, 33). A sample chart of a normal menstrual cycle 

### Author Manuscript

uncomplicated by underlying health conditions is included in Supplementary Materials.

The menstrual cycle also varies with intrinsic characteristics, especially age and body mass index (BMI). Cycle length, bleeding duration, and follicular phase length all decrease slightly with age during a menstruator’s 30s and early 40s (31, 35). In a study describing the characteristics of more than 600,000 menstrual cycles documented by 124,648 tracking app users, compared with the cycles of people aged 18–24 years, those aged 40–45 years had cycles that were on average 2.9 days shorter, with a 0.5-day shorter bleeding duration and a 

### Author Manuscript 3.2-day shorter follicular phase (31). Cycle variability also changes with age. FIGO suggests 

that variation in cycle length should be ≤9 days at age 19–25, ≤7 days at age 26–41, and ≤9 days at age 42–45 (30). Additionally, menstruators ≥30 years may have one less day of peak quality cervical fluid compared to those \<30 years old (27). Higher BMI (30–50 kg/m2) is associated with longer cycles, especially BMI \>50 kg/m2, and lower BMI (\<18.5 kg/m2) with increased cycle length variability (36, 37).

## Non-reproductive physiologic changes across the menstrual cycle The menstrual cycle impacts an array of non-reproductive physiologic processes in the body (Table 1). The classic vital signs – temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure – all measurably increase in the luteal phase before declining with the onset of menstruation (38–43). The implications of these changes for other physiologic functions are 

### Author Manuscript not well known, but at minimum are connected to cyclic changes in thermoregulation, fluid 

regulation, and metabolism.

Thermoregulation keeps body temperature stable across varying environments and activities. Increased luteal phase BBT increases the temperature thresholds triggering skin vasodilation, sweating, and shivering (39, 40, 44). Because large volumes of blood are moved to and from skin circulation as part of the body’s responses to heat and cold, blood pressure regulation is intertwined with thermoregulation. Cyclic blood pressure changes are thought to be linked to levels of estradiol – a vasodilator – and the sympathetic nervous system, which is more active in the luteal phase as evidenced by decreased heart rate variability (39, 41, 43).

### Estrogen and progesterone also influence fluid. Estrogen decreases the threshold for arginine Author Manuscript vasopressin (AVP) release; when AVP levels are higher, more water is resorbed by the 

kidneys rather than excreted in urine, resulting in lower plasma osmolality without water retention (45, 46). Progesterone is thought to increase aldosterone concentration, which promotes sodium reabsorption and water retention (45, 46). The implications of elevated luteal phase estrogen and progesterone for fluid dynamics is not completely understood, but in the luteal phase, plasma volume has been observed to decline up to 8%, and plasma osmolality and interstitial fluid are elevated (45, 46). Energy intake and the basal metabolic rate also increase in the luteal phase, accompanied by changes to carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism (38, 47).

Interactions between the immune system and the menstrual cycle are not yet fully understood. Although research on immune cells fluctuations across the cycle is scant, 

### Author Manuscript regulatory T cells appear to be lower in the luteal phase, and C-reactive protein (CRP), 

an indicator of general inflammation, is elevated during menses (48, 49). This aligns with observations that some autoimmune disorders have cyclic flare-ups, typically worsening premenstrually and during menses (49). Additionally, immune responses occurring with some vaccines, like the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, are hypothesized to impact the HPO axis, causing temporary changes to cycle length (50).

Circadian rhythms, hemostatic factors, and the vaginal microbiome also vary across the menstrual cycle. The amplitude of several circadian rhythms, including body temperature 

### Author Manuscript and cortisol levels, decreases during the luteal phase (51, 52). Circadian rhythms interact 

with the menstrual cycle in complex ways, including via the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the circadian pacemaker, which contains estrogen and progesterone receptors and interacts with GnRH across the menstrual cycle (53, 54). The complex neuroendocrine interplay between the menstrual cycle and circadian rhythms has primarily been studied in rodents and is not fully understood in humans. Although most hemostatic factors do not appear to fluctuate cyclically, evidence does support changes to von Willebrand factor and platelets, which are lowest during menses and the early follicular phase (55). Composition of the vaginal microbiome strongly varies across the menstrual cycle, with more species diversity and decreased _Lactobacillus_ spp. abundance during menses. As the cycle progresses, microbial composition stabilizes, with _Lactobacillus_ spp. dominance in the luteal phase.

### Our understanding of these cyclic changes is limited as menstruators are underrepresented Author Manuscript in physiologic research and menstrual cycle data is infrequently documented. Despite this, 

cyclic fluctuations of diverse physiologic parameters and processes have been observed, underscoring the importance and value of collecting menstrual cycle data and accounting for cycle phase in research.

## The menstrual cycle as health indicator The menstrual cycle responds to diverse health conditions (Table 2). Observed over time, it can serve as an indicator for endocrine diseases, bleeding disorders, structural anomalies of the reproductive tract, neoplasms, infections, and infertility (30, 58). While providing detailed clinical practice guidelines on using menstrual cycle characteristics to diagnose health conditions is beyond the scope of this review, practice guidelines and reviews with a clinical practice emphasis related to the menstrual cycle and cited in this review are listed by 

Author Manuscript topic in Table S1 for ease of further reference.

Irregular cycle length, variable volume and duration of bleeding, and amenorrhea indicate disturbances to the endocrine system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis (59, 60). Endocrine conditions that can perturb the menstrual cycle include type 1 and 2 diabetes (61), polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) (62, 63), thyroid diseases (64), primary or premature ovarian insufficiency (65, 66), and ovulatory disorders (19, 67). PCOS, experienced by 10–13% of menstruators, is a common cause of infertility, metabolic, and immune disorders (63). Features of PCOS menstrual cycles include anovulation or rare ovulation, long follicular phase, short luteal phase, intermenstrual bleeding, and an increased number of days with cervical fluid (68). Menstrual cycle tracking can aid in PCOS diagnosis and management (68, 69). Documenting menstrual cycle patterns can help assess, diagnose, 

Author Manuscript and treat infertility, guiding clinical testing and fertility treatment timing (21, 70).

Approximately 27%−53% of menstruators experience heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) (71, 72), or bleeding that affects quality of life (73). HMB can cause iron deficiency, anemia, fatigue, and hemodynamic instability (74, 75). It is often the sentinel symptom of a bleeding disorder (76, 77): studies found 29%−47% of menstruators with HMB have a bleeding disorder, most commonly von Willebrand’s disease or platelet dysfunction (78, 79). Bleeding disorders are underdiagnosed: on average the delay from first symptoms to diagnosis in menstruators is more than 10 years (73), underscoring the potential of 

### Author Manuscript menstrual cycle documentation to improve timely diagnosis. While rare, menstruators with 

bleeding disorders can experience hemorrhage during ovulation (80). A systematic review of hemostatic factors across the menstrual cycle found evidence for cyclic variation of platelets and von Willebrand factor, with lowest levels during menstruation and the early follicular phase (55). Anovulatory cycles from ovulation disorders such as PCOS also increase the risk of HMB, as prolonged estrogen exposure causes increased endometrial build-up and uncontrolled bleeding (81).

The menstrual cycle can indicate congenital structural/obstructive anomalies of the reproductive tract, with symptoms including amenorrhea, increasing dysmenorrhea after menarche, and pelvic or abdominal pain (cyclic and non-cyclic) from menstrual blood retention (82–84). Because structural differences are rarely externally visible, these symptoms during puberty often provide the first clue of an underlying condition (82). 

### Author Manuscript Other structural abnormalities causing HMB, dysmenorrhea, and intermenstrual bleeding 

are polyps, adenomyosis, fibroids, endometriosis, and neoplasms of the reproductive tract ranging from cervical dysplasia to uterine cancers (30).

Abnormal uterine bleeding like spotting and intermenstrual bleeding can signal sexually transmitted infections (30, 58). Chlamydia and gonorrhea, often asymptomatic, commonly cause cervicitis (85). Left untreated, they can develop into pelvic inflammatory disease (86, 87), increasing the risk of infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain (85, 88).

Shifts in menstrual cycle patterns can also signal physiologic changes such as pregnancy, lactational amenorrhea and the postpartum return of the ovarian cycle, perimenopause, and menopause (89, 90). When people are familiar with their menstrual cycle patterns, these 

### Author Manuscript changes can be detected earlier and better understood. The postpartum transition to regular 

menstrual cycles begins with amenorrhea followed by a gradual resumption of ovarian activity (89, 91). Ovulation may or may not occur prior to first menses. After menstruation resumes, the first cycles may be anovulatory and are usually irregular with long follicular phases and short luteal phases (\<6 days) that gradually lengthen, eventually transitioning to fertile ovulatory cycles, with about 85% ovulatory cycles by fourth cycles occurring more than 12 months postpartum (91, 92). Among people breastfeeding, the mean first ovulation occurs at 206–322 days postpartum, and the mean first menses at 225–289 days (91, 93, 94). The length of the first postpartum menstrual cycle can be quite variable – 21 to 55 days – with cycle length falling to a more regular 26–35 days by the third postpartum cycle (93). During postpartum amenorrhea and the transition to regular menstrual cycles, cervical fluid patterns and hormonal profiles (urinary estrogen and pregnanediol) are poorly correlated, presenting challenges when tracking signs of ovarian activity (91, 92).

### Author Manuscript

During perimenopause – the 1 to 5 years before menopause – ovarian follicles are depleted and ovarian estrogen and progesterone production declines, resulting in more variable, longer, anovulatory cycles (95). The early menopausal transition is characterized by increasingly variable cycle lengths of ≥7 days difference from an individual’s typical cycles (96). In the late menopausal transition, there are ≥2 skipped cycles and an intermenstrual interval of ≥60 days (96). The duration and volume of menstrual bleeding also becomes more variable; increased bleeding can occur especially in ovulatory cycles in the late 

### Author Manuscript menopausal transition (96, 97). Postmenopause is dated retrospectively after an individual 

has experienced 12 months of amenorrhea (96).

## Menstrual cycle impacts on health conditions Hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle can cause cyclic changes in neurological, chronic pain, mental health, gastrointestinal, and other conditions (Table 1) (98). Documenting the menstrual cycle can help identify cyclic flare-ups, aiding in symptom control. Migraine headaches and seizures are more common perimenstrually and around ovulation. Estrogen withdrawal is thought to contribute to migraines, as estrogen receptors are found throughout the brain, including along pain-processing and migraine pathways (99, 100). Estrogen-progesterone ratios may impact menstrual-related epilepsy, increasing perimenstrual seizures: estrogen is a proconvulsant while progesterone has an anticonvulsant 

### Author Manuscript effect (101, 102).

Several autoimmune diseases exhibit cyclic symptom fluctuations. Systemic lupus erythematosus may have premenstrual flare-ups (49). Multiple sclerosis symptoms also worsen premenstrually, with an increased lesion size observed in the luteal phase. These changes are hypothesized to be linked to increased luteal phase BBT, as temperature elevations can exacerbate neurological symptoms in demyelinating conditions (101). In contrast, rheumatoid arthritis symptoms typically improve during the luteal phase when estrogen and progesterone are high, with increased pain and stiffness during menses and the early follicular phase (98, 103). Chronic pain associated with fibromyalgia musculoskeletal conditions, and temporomandibular disorder also can worsen perimenstrually when estrogen, involved along pain pathways, is low (103).

### Author Manuscript Some mental health disorders can be exacerbated by the menstrual cycle. Symptoms of 

depression and eating, panic, borderline personality, bipolar, and psychotic disorders all may worsen during the perimenstrual phase, possibly due to some individuals’ increased sensitivity to hormone fluctuations (104). Asthma worsens perimenstrually, possibly related to declining progesterone, although the etiology remains largely unknown (98, 105). In irritable bowel syndrome, progesterone relaxes sphincters and delays gastric emptying, with constipation after ovulation when progesterone is high, and perimenstrual loose stool when levels fall (98).

## Menstrual cycle characteristics and long-term health Menstrual cycle characteristics – especially long and irregular cycles – are associated with long-term health outcomes, including cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), 

Author Manuscript fracture, and premature mortality (Table 3).

Prolonged estrogen and decreased progesterone exposure, as may occur with long, irregular cycles and amenorrhea, might increase the risk of cancer (106). Two prospective cohort studies (n=94,471) found that compared to participants with regular cycles, those with irregular cycles had 11% (95% CI: 2%, 21%) increased risk of any cancer, 23% (95% CI: 9%, 39%) increased risk of obesity-related cancer (106), and were more than twice as likely to develop ovarian cancer starting at age 70 (HR=2.26; 95% CI: 1.20, 4.26) (107). 

### Author Manuscript Menstruators with long cycles also had a 22% (95% CI: 9%, 27%) increased risk of any 

cancer compared to those with 26- to 31-day cycles (106).

Five cohort studies (n=842,616) examined associations between menstrual cycle characteristics and diabetes (108–111). In irregular and long cycles, FSH and LH stimulate ovarian androgen production, which increases insulin resistance and may lead to diabetes (109). Compared to participants with regular cycles, those with irregular cycles had 17% −66% increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes (108, 109, 111, 112), and were 1.5 times more likely to develop gestational diabetes (110). Menstruators whose cycles took more than 5 years to reach regularity after menarche had a 47% (95% CI: 13%, 89%) increased risk of type 1 diabetes and a 24% (95% CI: 5%, 33%) risk of type 2 diabetes compared to those whose cycles were regular within 4 years of menarche (112). Long cycles were associated with 37%−50% increased risk of type 2 diabetes (109), and 42% (95% 

### Author Manuscript CI: 15%, 75%) increased risk of gestational diabetes (110). Although associations between 

long and irregular cycles and diabetes could be driven by conditions like PCOS, two of the largest cohort studies found associations between cycle irregularity and frequency and type 2 diabetes were unchanged (108) or remained significant (112) when the analysis was restricted to individuals without a PCOS diagnosis.

Studies within the same cohorts (n=894,850) examined associations between the menstrual cycle and CVD (108, 111–114), as cycle dysfunction can impact glucose homeostasis leading to hyperinsulinemia and elevated testosterone levels, CVD risk factors (108). Additionally, because estrogen reduces vascular inflammation, cycles with lower estrogen levels could contribute to atherosclerotic CVD (108). Compared to regular cycles, irregular cycles were associated with 8%−40% increased risk of CVD (108, 111, 113, 114), and 

### Author Manuscript cycle length extremes (long or short cycles) were associated with 11–44% increased risk 

of CVD (108, 113, 114). While conditions like PCOS could help explain associations between menstrual cycle characteristics and CVD (115), two studies found that associations between irregular cycles and CVD changed minimally (108) or attenuated but remained significant (112) when the analysis included only participants without PCOS. Many of the same neuro-endocrine pathways regulating ovarian function also impact bone remodeling and homeostasis (116). Estrogen inhibits bone resorption, reducing bone remodeling, while progesterone stimulates bone formation (116, 117). In estrogen-deficient states – such as functional hypothalamic amenorrhea with low estrogen – bone resorption outpaces bone production, accelerating bone loss (116, 117). Because 90% of bone mass is achieved by age 18, amenorrhea and low estrogen during adolescence can irreversibly impair bone density, increasing risks of osteoporosis and fracture (117, 118). Irregular cycles, long 

### Author Manuscript cycles, variable bleeding duration, and age at menarche are all associated with increased risk 

of postmenopausal fracture (119, 120). Reviews on lactational amenorrhea indicate that bone mineral density declines during lactation to provide calcium for the breastfeeding infant but recovers after breastfeeding is finished (121, 122), and breastfeeding duration is not associated with increased postmenopausal fracture risk (121).

Only one study (n=79,505) has examined cycle characteristics and premature mortality (\<70 years) (123). Irregular and long cycles were associated with 18%−40% increased risk of 

### Author Manuscript death depending on the age when the cycle characteristic was observed, with higher risk for 

older menstruators.

Collectively, evidence indicates cycle characteristics are associated with long-term health outcomes, with the potential to inform preventive screening and behavior modifications to address long-term health risks. While it is possible that undiagnosed PCOS may help explain some associations between cycle characteristics and CVD and type 2 diabetes, evidence suggests menstruators with long and irregular cycles may be at increased risk of long-term cardiometabolic outcomes independent of PCOS (108, 112). Notably, cycle phenotype transitions from regular to irregular or normal to longer cycles from young- to mid-adulthood were associated with increased risks of cancer, CVD, diabetes, and gestational diabetes (106, 109, 110, 114), underscoring the importance of tracking cycle characteristics over time.

## Optimizing wellness with the menstrual cycle Numerous self-help books and popular press articles frame menstruation as natural, manageable, and potentially empowering: by understanding and aligning their menstrual cycles with their lifestyle, diet, and exercise, menstruators can improve their physical and emotional wellbeing (124). In this section, we review evidence for using the menstrual cycle to optimize reproductive health, exercise, and nutrition.

The primary reasons menstruators report cycle tracking include optimizing reproductive health through conceiving or avoiding pregnancy, improving body literacy, and informing conversations with healthcare providers (125). People who track their menstrual cycles have higher levels of knowledge about fertility (126), and menstrual cycle tracking app 

### Author Manuscript use increases conception rates for pregnancy planners, particularly when combined with 

additional fertility indicators like BBT or cervical fluid (70, 127, 128). Cycle tracking is also used for contraception. Only the Natural Cycles app is FDA-approved as a contraceptive device and currently available, with a 13-cycle typical use pregnancy probability of 7.2% (95% CI: 6.4%, 8.1%) (129).

Hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle influence metabolism, thirst, fluid regulation, and thermoregulation, offering a plausible physiologic basis for efforts to optimize nutrition and exercise across the cycle (Table 1). Evidence suggests daily energy intake is lowest during the late follicular phase and ovulation and increases during the luteal phase (47, 130). Estrogen may promote muscle strength by increasing glycogen uptake and has antioxidant properties that could protect against exercise-induced muscle damage and 

Author Manuscript inflammation (131), potentially improving follicular phase resistance training efficacy (132).

However, data are lacking to support cycle phase-specific nutritional and exercise interventions at the population level (130, 133). A meta-analysis of 78 studies found exercise performance might be slightly reduced during menses compared to other menstrual cycle phases (ES0.5 = −0.06; 95% CrI: −0.16, 0.04) (131). Hypotheses that high pre-ovulatory estrogen concentrations could contribute to ligament laxity and increased mid-cycle injuries are not supported by high quality research (134). Reviews on athlete nutrition concluded that 

### Author Manuscript simply having adequate energy intake was crucial, as inadequate intake can cause functional 

hypothalamic amenorrhea (133, 135).

While generalized recommendations related to menstrual cycle phase, physical exercise, and nutrition are not currently supported by scientific literature, surveys of athletes found that nearly 1/3 reported their menstrual cycle impacted training and performance (136). Accordingly, individuals can track their cycles along with training, recovery, and other symptoms to determine personal patterns, needs, and strategies (135). The lack of data supporting cycle-related nutrition and exercise interventions reflects an underrepresentation of menstruators and a lack of menstrual cycle data collection in nutrition and exercise studies, leading to gaps in basic knowledge about the whole-body impact of menstrual cycles (47, 131, 135).

## Author ManuscriptExternal exposures influence the menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle responds to diverse environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, and intrinsic characteristics (Table 4) – including age, air pollution, and night-shift work – that interact with neuro-endocrine pathways.

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) interfere with endocrine system function via multiple pathways. Common exposure sources include personal care products, food, water, dust, industrial chemicals, and pesticides (138, 139). Recent reviews found per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) were associated with longer, more irregular cycles (140), while bisphenol A (BPA), butyl paraben, select phthalates, and flame retardant polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) were associated with a shorter luteal phase (36, 141, 142). Metals also can have endocrine-disrupting effects: cadmium and selenium are 

### Author Manuscript associated with decreased cycle length, copper with increased cycle length, and mercury and 

lead with irregular cycles (36, 143). Air pollution has been observed to increase irregular cycles and follicular phase length and decrease luteal phase length (36, 142).

Lifestyle factors also influence menstrual cycles. Low vitamin D has been associated with prolonged and irregular menstrual cycles, even in those with no history of PCOS (144–146). Vitamin D is obtained from supplements or synthesized in the skin with sunlight exposure. A meta-analysis of 8 studies (n=28,479) found night-shift work disrupted circadian rhythms, increasing the odds of cycle disruption (OR 1.15; 95% CI 1.01, 1.31) (147). Sedentary behavior and high alcohol consumption were associated with increased cycle irregularity (148), and smoking, caffeine, and high alcohol intake with shorter cycles and HMB (36, 148, 149).

Author Manuscript Recently, studies have examined menstrual cycle responses to COVID-19-related exposures. 

A meta-analysis of 4 studies (n=25,054) compared cycle characteristics before and after COVID-19 vaccination; vaccinated menstruators were more likely to report temporary cycle changes (OR=1.91, 95% CI 1.76, 2.07) (150). A large cohort study (n=9,652, 128,094 cycles) found vaccination was associated with a 1–2 day increase in cycle length and an increased odds of a long cycle (\>38 days), especially with the Johnson & Johnson vaccine; cycles quickly returned to pre-vaccination lengths (50). Two systematic reviews found COVID-19 infection was associated with longer cycles and a possible decrease in 

### Author Manuscript menstrual bleeding, but the reviewed studies were small and lacked pre-infection data for 

comparison (151, 152). Pandemic conditions also were associated with cycle changes: a meta-analysis of 7 studies (n=21,729) found that compared to non-lockdown times, lockdowns were associated with an increased odds of changes in menstrual cycle length (OR=9.14; 95% CI 3.16, 26.5) (153). Another review of 17 studies found that during the pandemic, menstruators reported increased menstrual irregularities, cycle variability, heavier bleeding, and dysmenorrhea, with participants reporting higher levels of anxiety, stress, and depression more likely to experience cycle changes (152).

## Documenting the menstrual cycle as a vital sign Clinicians and researchers should ask patients about their menstrual cycle at least once to identify unusual cycles or acute issues. Ideally, information should be documented over 

### Author Manuscript multiple cycles. While data from a single cycle can be used to address an acute issue, 

patterns over time offer additional insights, as with other vital signs, and may be used to diagnose chronic conditions such as PCOS or endometriosis. Clinicians and researchers should educate patients in basic menstrual cycle biology and tracking skills so they can track, observe, and identify patterns in their own cycles over time (see Supplementary Materials, educational handouts, menstrual cycle tracking chart). By tracking their cycles, menstruators have a powerful tool to understand and optimize their own health and wellness, and to access timely treatment and preventative care.

Tracking the menstrual cycle is simple and involves noting dates and intensity of vaginal bleeding. This allows determination of cycle length and bleeding duration and intensity. When multiple cycles are documented, cycle regularity and variability can also be observed. Signs such as BBT and cervical fluid can allow retrospective identification of ovulation 

### Author Manuscript and calculation of follicular and luteal phase lengths. At-home urinary hormone monitoring 

kits can also be used to identify ovulation, the fertile window, and phase lengths. Other symptoms (mood, pain, headaches), activities (sex, travel, exercise), and information (diet, medications) can also be recorded to understand how the menstrual cycle may be impacting health conditions, and how activities might impact menstrual cycle patterns.

The menstrual cycle can be documented on calendars, special charts (see Supplementary Materials, educational handouts, menstrual cycle tracking chart), or using smartphone-based apps. Apps range from simple calendars to proprietary algorithms predicting menses onset, the fertile window, and ovulation. Many apps allow users to document symptoms and activities (154). Some integrate temperature monitoring devices – including rings, arm- bands, and bracelets – to facilitate BBT tracking (155). Users report apps improve self-

### Author Manuscript knowledge, health management, and information-sharing with healthcare providers (125, 

156).

Despite tracking app popularity, reviews have found their algorithms for next cycle or fertile window prediction to be inaccurate, particularly for users with irregular cycles, and that 20% of reviewed apps contained incorrect clinical information (3, 157–159). Few available apps incorporate evidence-based fertility awareness methods, even though 33% are marketed for contraception and 15% for conception (159).

### Author Manuscript Data privacy is a growing concern, as many apps allow third-party access to users’ 

personal information (160), which can be used without consent for advertising, research, and insurance purposes, or shared with employers (161). Health apps’ data collection and privacy practices are not regulated unless they are considered medical devices (161). In the wake of the United States Supreme Court’s 2022 _Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization_ decision, which eliminated the constitutional right to abortion, some app users were concerned that information from period tracking apps could be used to prosecute illegal abortions, especially self-managed at-home abortions which are clinically indistinguishable from miscarriages. However, based on case reviews and experience, several legal experts suggest that texts, emails, and search/website histories are more likely to be used than apps as digital evidence in abortion prosecutions (162). Apps that store data locally on smartphones are more secure (162).

### Author Manuscript Development of a standardized menstrual history questionnaire could improve care and 

facilitate research (163). In primary care settings, menstrual histories should include an assessment of bleeding characteristics, associated symptoms, impacts on quality of life, and a comprehensive medical history (Table 5) (163). Cycle tracking could complement history-taking. In preparation for a routine health visit, patients could track their cycles for 3–6 months, with charts reviewed at the visit. Alternatively, patients could track their cycles after a visit when potential health concerns or goals have been identified and discuss in follow-up care.

# Conclusion

### Documenting the menstrual cycle as well as consideration of symptoms in relation to the Author Manuscript menstrual cycle can inform the diagnosis, assessment, and management of health conditions 

commonly encountered in primary and specialized care settings. However, research gaps and challenges in integrating its documentation into clinical practice remain.

While much evidence supports the menstrual cycle as a vital sign, more research is needed on its associations with long-term outcomes, external exposures, and wellness. External exposures that are associated with small, seemingly insignificant shifts in cycle characteristics on an individual level could result in population-wide shifts toward extremes of cycle length and variability, with major clinical and health implications. Research suggests strong connections between cycle characteristics and long-term health outcomes, but the etiology is not well understood. Future work could explore using cycle characteristics as biomarkers of future risk, guiding preventive care strategies (164). Menstruators already use cycle self-knowledge to improve their health and wellbeing. 

### Author Manuscript Research comprehensively characterizing the menstrual cycle’s influences on physiologic 

processes throughout the body (165) and high-quality studies on wellness-oriented interventions are needed.

Although apps abound, tools for cycle tracking are not integrated into clinical practice. A standardized menstrual history tool incorporating cycle tracking capabilities into electronic medical record systems and simple ways for patients to share app data with providers would facilitate menstrual cycle documentation. As a start, providers can share the tracking and 

### Author Manuscript educational tool included in the Supplementary Materials with their patients. Additionally, 

guidelines on the use of menstrual cycle data for clinical decision-making are needed. In a research context, several papers have suggested recommendations for studying both the menstrual cycle and its impact on other outcomes of interest, and evaluated different approaches to measuring changes in cycle characteristics (166–169). Guidelines on best practices for menstrual cycle data collection and analysis are also needed to improve research quality and enable study comparisons, with implications for research across disciplines.

Because of its broad scope, this review has limitations. For example, discussing the impact of exposures for which there is _in vivo_ evidence or few studies, but no systematic reviews, is beyond this paper’s scope. Additionally, like other vital signs, the menstrual cycle is frequently altered. Discussion of interventions altering or suppressing the menstrual and 

### Author Manuscript ovarian cycles such as hormonal contraceptives, gender-affirming hormone treatment, and 

ovary-sparing hysterectomy is beyond the scope of this review. Given the high importance of such interventions, research focusing on, for example, post-hysterectomy ovarian function 

(170) and menstrual cycle responses to hormone therapy (171) is warranted.

Treating and documenting the menstrual cycle as a vital sign has the potential to transform research and clinical care, promoting menstrual health equity and, most fundamentally, improving the health and wellbeing of people who menstruate.

### Author Manuscript Essential Points

- The menstrual cycle is a vital sign that is associated with health outcomes and can be used to identify pathologies, indicate the need for treatment and 

monitor a clinical course, and promote wellness.

- Clinicians and researchers should ask patients about their menstrual cycle at least once, to identify unusual cycles or acute issues, but ideally the menstrual 

cycle should be tracked longitudinally over time, just like other vital signs.

- Clinicians and researchers should educate patients in basic menstrual cycle biology and tracking skills so they can track, observe, and identify patterns in 

their own cycles over time.

### Author Manuscript • Tracking the menstrual cycle is a powerful tool for understanding 

gynecological and general health, with the potential to improve preventive 

and primary care delivery and allow menstruators to understand and optimize 

their own health and wellness.
